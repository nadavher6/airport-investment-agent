# Traffic & Demand — Investment Diligence Guide

> **Context:** This is section 2 of the airport investment criteria framework.
> Traffic & demand is the **foundation** of any airport investment thesis — every
> revenue line (landing fees, PFCs, parking, concessions) ultimately derives from
> how many people and planes move through the airport, and **how durable** that
> flow is. The goal is to judge not just *how much* demand exists, but *how high
> quality and resilient* it is.

---

## Quick reference

| # | Criterion | Core question | 🟢 Green flag | 🔴 Red flag |
|---|-----------|---------------|--------------|-------------|
| 1 | Enplanements (total + trend) | How big and growing? | Steady CAGR above national avg | Declining / stagnant |
| 2 | O&D vs. Connecting mix | Is demand *local* or *borrowed*? | High O&D % | High connecting %, one airline |
| 3 | Hub type & carrier concentration | Who controls the traffic? | Balanced mix / committed hub | Single mobile carrier |
| 4 | Catchment demographics & growth | Is the demand engine growing? | Growing, affluent metro | Shrinking, single-industry |
| 5 | Load factors & frequency | Is latent demand there? | Full planes, rising frequency | Low loads, cuts |
| 6 | International vs. domestic split | How premium is the mix? | Growing long-haul / gateway | Domestic short-haul only |
| 7 | Seasonality & volatility | How stable is the cash flow? | Low seasonality, resilient | Single-season / cyclical |

## 1. Enplanements (total + trend)

**What it is:** An *enplanement* = one passenger **boarding** an aircraft (a
departure). It is the industry's standard volume unit. "Total passengers" ≈ 2×
enplanements, since that counts arrivals too — always check which a source means.

**How it's measured:** The FAA publishes annual enplanement data and sorts airports
into size categories:
- **Large Hub** — ≥ 1% of total U.S. enplanements
- **Medium Hub** — 0.25%–1%
- **Small Hub** — 0.05%–0.25%
- **Nonhub** — < 0.05% but still served by scheduled service

> ⚠️ The FAA "hub" label is a **size bucket**, not a connecting-hub concept. Don't
> confuse it with criterion #3.

**Why it matters:** Volume drives nearly every revenue stream — landing fees, PFCs
(per-passenger), parking, concessions. More enplanements = bigger, more defensible
cash flow.

**What to look at:**
- Absolute size (Large / Medium / Small hub)
- **5–10 year CAGR** — growing, flat, or declining?
- **Recovery shape** post-COVID (back to 2019 levels? faster/slower than national avg?)

- 🟢 Steady multi-year growth above the national average.
- 🔴 Declining or stagnant enplanements = shrinking base, hard to underwrite expansion.

## 2. O&D vs. Connecting mix

**What it is:** The crucial quality split of the passenger base.
- **O&D (Origin & Destination):** passengers whose trip *starts or ends* at this
  airport — real local demand.
- **Connecting:** passengers just *changing planes*; the airport is a transfer
  point, not their destination.

**Why it matters — the single most important demand-quality metric:**
- **O&D is "sticky" and high-yield.** Local demand exists regardless of airline
  strategy. People in that metro *need* to fly; you cannot easily move that demand.
- **Connecting traffic is "borrowed."** It exists only because an airline *chose*
  to route its network through this hub. If the airline de-hubs, that traffic can
  **vanish overnight** — the airport has no control over it.
- O&D passengers also spend more on-site (they park, they are not rushing to a
  connection), so **non-aeronautical revenue per O&D passenger is far higher**.

**Data source:** DOT/BTS **DB1B** (Airline Origin & Destination Survey).

- 🟢 High O&D % = resilient, locally anchored demand (e.g., LAX, JFK, SFO).
- 🔴 High connecting % = dependent on one airline's hub decision.

> **Cautionary tales:** Pittsburgh (US Airways de-hubbed), Cincinnati/CVG and
> Memphis (Delta de-hubbed) — airports built massive terminals for connecting
> traffic that then disappeared, leaving stranded capex and empty gates.

## 3. Hub type & carrier concentration

**What it is:** Who controls the traffic.
- A **fortress hub** = one airline runs > 50–70% of flights (Delta/Atlanta,
  United/Houston, American/Charlotte, Delta/Minneapolis).
- An **origin / O&D market** = diverse carrier mix serving local demand.

**Why it matters — the dominance paradox:**
- A dominant carrier brings **volume, frequency, and connectivity** — good for the
  raw traffic numbers.
- But it creates **concentration risk**: the airport's fate is tied to one airline's
  health and strategy. A bankruptcy, merger, or strategic pivot can gut the base.
  The airline also gains **pricing power** over the airport (resists fee increases,
  can threaten to leave).

**What to measure:** carrier market share (HHI concentration), number of carriers,
and **LCC/ULCC presence** (Southwest, Spirit, Frontier add competition and resilience).

- 🟢 Balanced carrier mix, *or* a committed fortress hub (airline owns facilities,
  holds long leases, is deeply invested).
- 🔴 Single carrier with no commitment and mobile (connecting) traffic.

## 4. Catchment demographics & economic growth

**What it is:** The **catchment area** = the region that realistically uses this
airport (typically a ~60–90 min drive radius, adjusted for competing airports). It
is the demand engine behind O&D traffic.

**Why it matters:** O&D demand is a function of **how many people live there, how
wealthy they are, and how much business / tourism the region generates.** Air travel
demand correlates strongly with **regional GDP and population growth**.

**What to look at:**
- **Population** size and growth rate (growing Sun Belt vs. declining Rust Belt)
- **Income / GDP per capita** — wealthier catchment = more trips, premium travel, more spend
- **Corporate HQs & business base** — drives high-yield business travel and premium cabins
- **Tourism / leisure draw** — inbound demand (Orlando, Las Vegas); more volatile but large
- **Competing airports** splitting the catchment (NYC or LA multi-airport systems)

- 🟢 Growing, affluent metro with corporate + tourism demand.
- 🔴 Shrinking population, single-industry economy, strong competing airport nearby.

---

## 5. Load factors & frequency

**What it is:**
- **Load factor** = % of available seats actually filled (passengers ÷ seats).
  Industry averages now run ~80–85%.
- **Frequency** = number of flights per route per day (e.g., 8× daily to Chicago
  vs. 1× daily).

**Why it matters:**
- **High load factors + high frequency = latent unmet demand.** If planes are
  consistently full and airlines keep adding frequencies, the market wants more than
  can currently be supplied — the signal that **capacity expansion would be absorbed**
  (the core of a modernization / expansion thesis).
- **Frequency** is also a quality indicator: frequent service to many destinations =
  a mature, valuable market. Business travelers pay premiums for frequency.

**Data source:** BTS **T-100** (segment traffic: seats, passengers, flights).

- 🟢 Persistently high load factors + growing frequencies + route additions.
- 🔴 Low load factors / airlines cutting frequency = soft demand, no case for capacity.

## 6. International vs. domestic split (long-haul / widebody share)

**What it is:** The mix of domestic, short-haul international (Mexico / Caribbean),
and **long-haul international** (transatlantic / transpacific widebody) flights. This
is the one factor the current agent already weights (~20%).

**Why it matters:**
- **International & long-haul = premium economics.** Widebody flights carry more
  passengers, pay higher landing fees (fee ∝ aircraft weight), and bring **high-spend
  international passengers** (duty-free, premium lounges, long dwell time → more
  concession revenue).
- International service requires — and justifies — **infrastructure investment**:
  Federal Inspection Services (customs / immigration halls), widebody gates, longer
  runways. That is exactly the modernization capex a PE investor funds.
- International connectivity signals **gateway status** that is hard to replicate (a moat).

- 🟢 Growing international / long-haul share, gateway status, widebody operations.
- 🔴 Purely domestic short-haul with no international growth path = capped revenue quality.

---

## 7. Seasonality & volatility (demand resilience)

**What it is:** How **stable** the traffic is — across the calendar year and across
economic cycles.

**Why it matters:** Cash-flow *quality*, not just quantity. Infra investors prize
**predictable, resilient** cash flows.
- **Seasonality:** leisure airports (Orlando, ski / beach markets) swing hard by
  season — peaks strain capacity, troughs leave assets idle. Business / O&D markets
  are smoother.
- **Cyclical volatility:** how did traffic hold up in **2008 and COVID**? Business
  travel collapses in recessions; leisure and VFR (visiting friends / relatives) is
  more resilient. Resilience in downturns = lower risk premium = higher valuation multiple.

- 🟢 Low seasonality, diversified demand (business + leisure + VFR), fast recovery.
- 🔴 Single-season leisure dependence or deep cyclical swings.

---

## How these connect for the investment thesis

The art is reading these **together**, because they interact:

> **High O&D %** + **growing affluent catchment** + **high load factors / frequency**
> + **growing international share** + **low volatility** = the gold-standard demand
> profile: resilient, high-yield, with a clear case that **expansion capex will be
> filled by real local demand**.

> Conversely, large raw enplanements that are mostly **connecting traffic for one
> airline** is a *trap* — it looks big, but the demand is not the airport's to keep.

### Gap vs. the current agent

The current agent collapses demand into **congestion + unmet demand + long-haul**.
The highest-value **missing signals** in this section are:

1. **O&D vs. connecting** (quality / stickiness of demand)
2. **Catchment growth** (durability of demand)

Adding those two would improve the agent's judgment more than anything else in the
traffic & demand dimension.
